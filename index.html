Learning to study like a professional

Reflective post on my academic journey

When I started at BAC, I assumed that university would feel like school with harder content. I was wrong. The biggest change was not the difficulty of the work but the amount of responsibility that moved onto me. Nobody reminds you to revise, and nobody checks whether you have understood a topic until a test or assignment shows that you have not.

My situation made this more demanding. I am studying full time while helping to run Frame & Frqnc, where filming days, client messages and content deadlines do not wait for exam timetables. In the first weeks I tried to fit everything in by working late and hoping for the best. That left me tired and my notes thin. I realised I was reacting to deadlines instead of managing them.

The first change I made was to plan my week in writing. I now list assignment due dates, lectures and client commitments in one place and block out study sessions the way I would block out a shoot. Treating study time as a booking I cannot cancel has helped me protect it. The second change was to study in short, focused sessions with breaks, which keeps my concentration better than long sessions that drift.

Studying data analytics has also shown me that I learn best by doing. Reading about a concept helps, but I understand it properly only when I apply it to a small data set or explain it to someone else. This is why I now finish each topic by writing a short summary in my own words and testing myself on it.

I still have weaknesses. I sometimes underestimate how long written work takes, and I find it hard to say no when a business task arrives close to a deadline. My aim for the coming semester is to start assignments earlier, ask lecturers questions sooner and keep one evening a week free of both business and study so that I can rest.

This semester has taught me that academic success is a skill that can be built, not a talent I either have or lack. A growth mindset helps me treat a poor result as information. If a plan fails, I adjust it and try again, which is exactly how I would approach a data problem.

What my group taught me about teamwork

Reflection on group work using Tuckman's stages of group development

  1. FormingWe met, shared strengths and agreed the task.
  2. StormingDifferent ideas and working styles caused friction.
  3. NormingWe set roles, deadlines and a communication routine.
  4. PerformingWe worked efficiently and supported each other.
  5. AdjourningWe submitted, reviewed and reflected.

Forming

Our group was formed for a group assignment in my first semester. I knew some of my group mates only by sight, so the first meeting was polite and cautious. We introduced ourselves, shared what each person was comfortable doing, and read the brief together. Everyone was keen, but nobody wanted to take charge, which meant that very little was decided at that stage.

Storming

Disagreements appeared once we began planning. We had different ideas about how to approach the task, and some members preferred to work late at night while others needed earlier deadlines. A few messages in our group chat were misread as criticism. I noticed that I could become impatient when decisions took too long, because I am used to making quick calls in my business. We resolved this by pausing the chat discussion and meeting to talk it through. Hearing each other's tone and reasons made it easier to see that we wanted the same result.

Norming

After that meeting we agreed on working rules. We divided the task according to each person's strengths, set internal deadlines a few days before the real one, and chose one channel for updates. We also agreed that feedback should be specific and kind. These small routines gave the group a shared sense of how we worked.

Performing

In the performing stage we worked with much less friction. People completed their parts on time, asked for help early and reviewed each other's work before it was combined. Meetings became shorter because we arrived prepared. I took the lead on bringing the sections together and checking that the document read as one voice.

Adjourning

When we submitted, we held a short review of what worked and what we would change. The ending felt positive, although I realised we could have started the final check earlier. We shared our contact details so that we could continue to support each other in later modules.

What I learned

I learned that conflict is a normal part of group development and that it becomes useful when it is handled openly. I also learned that clear communication matters more than talent: written messages need context and a respectful tone, and difficult points are often better handled by speaking. Finally, I learned to listen before pushing my own idea. These lessons apply directly to my work at Frame & Frqnc, where every project depends on collaboration with clients and teammates.

Study tips that work for me

Strategies I use to keep up with a degree and a business.

Pomodoro sessions

Twenty-five minutes of focus, then a five-minute break. After four rounds I take a longer rest.

Flashcards

I put definitions and formulas on cards and test myself, which shows me quickly what I do not know yet.

Weekly plan

Every Sunday I list deadlines and book study time the way I book a shoot.

Teach it back

I explain a topic aloud in my own words. If I cannot, I go back to my notes.

Practise with data

I try each new Excel technique on a small data set before I move on.

Ask early

I raise questions with lecturers soon after a class, while the topic is fresh.
